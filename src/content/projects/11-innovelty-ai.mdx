## The problem

The paper-and-patent filing boom turned novelty checking into a full-time job. You search
IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar and Google Patents, you scroll through millions of papers a
year, and you identify the relevant ones by hand — a process that is tedious, easy to get
wrong, and one most people are not trained to do. The question a student actually has is
simple: *is this project idea original?* Without a clear guide, that is a hard
determination to make.

The academic and industry answer is prior-art analysis, and the tooling for it was
scattered. The system brings the sources together and does the reading.

## The pipeline

**1 · Collect.** Five sources, each behind its own API — arXiv (Atom), IEEE Xplore
(REST), DOAJ (API v2), Google Patents and Google Scholar (SearchAPI / SerpAPI) — are
queried for the idea's key phrases and normalised into one corpus dataframe that keeps
the papers and the patents distinguishable.

**2 · Embed.** Every record is encoded with `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, a 384-dimensional sentence
embedding, L2-normalised so that cosine similarity is a plain dot product.

**3 · Index.** The embeddings go into a FAISS `IndexFlatIP` flat inner-product index,
written once to disk and reused across searches. Because the vectors are normalised, the
inner product *is* cosine similarity — no separate normalisation step at query time. The
admin dashboard can rebuild the index from the current cache without a full re-scrape.

**4 · Retrieve.** The candidate idea is embedded with the same model and matched against
the index, pulling the top 50 neighbours. Retrieved hits get a **recency boost**: a paper
from 2020 onwards gains up to +0.05 on its score, so recent work is not buried under a
decade of older papers that happen to use similar vocabulary.

**5 · Score.** Novelty and confidence are computed separately, because they answer
different questions:

```
novelty   = (1 − max_similarity) × 100 − 3 × (hits with similarity > 0.7)
confidence = 50 + min(50, hits/50 × 30) + (1 / dispersion) × 10
```

The strongest match sets the ceiling on novelty, and every additional strong hit is
penalised, so an idea that collides with five papers is not scored like one that collides
with one. Confidence instead grows with how much evidence there is and how tightly that
evidence agrees — a 90% novelty score on a thin corpus never reads like a 90% score on a
full one. At **70% or above** the system says a patent can be filed; below that it says
to write it up as research instead.

**6 · Attribute.** A grounded Gemini pass (via LangChain) reads the retrieved patents and
papers alongside the idea and explains the score: which part of the idea is genuinely
new, which claim collides with which prior art, and what would have to change.

## What it produces

A novelty score with its confidence band, the similar patents and the similar papers
behind both, an AI chat grounded in the same retrieved corpus, and a PDF or CSV export of
the whole analysis. Search history is kept locally so a previous idea can be revisited.
There is no separate corpus for papers and for patents — the index spans both, which is
the point: a novelty question that only searches papers answers a different question than
the one being asked.

## The app

![The novelty checker — pipeline steps and idea input](/images/projects/screens/innovelty-ai.webp)

Six steps from an idea to a filing decision: search five sources, score it, show what it
collides with, chat about it, and export it.

![Idea input form filled with a sample description](/images/projects/screens/innovelty-ai-2.webp)

The idea is entered in plain language — title, domain, the problem, the approach and the
technical detail — and key phrases are extracted from it to drive the search.

![Admin dashboard — index rebuild from cache](/images/projects/screens/innovelty-ai-3.webp)

The admin view manages the corpus and rebuilds the FAISS index from the cached data
without a full re-scrape.
