## The appraisal is a graph, not a pipeline

Most of the work in a credit file is independent of the rest, and the sequence is wrong if you
model it as a straight line. The workflow is nine nodes in two parallel groups:

```
doc_parse ──┬─ fin_spread ──┐
            └─ gst_verify ──┤
                           ├─► promoter_intel ─► risk_score ─► cam_gen
gstr_engine ┬─ buyer_engine ┘                          └─► counter_fact
```

Spreading and GST verification run together; reconciliation and buyer-concentration run
together; everything downstream waits on both. The front end renders the graph as it runs,
with per-agent progress, so the analyst watches the file assemble rather than staring at a
spinner.

## Reconciliation is the part that earns the file

The GSTR-2A vs GSTR-3B view is the core: eight quarters per borrower, each carrying the
supplier's claimed ITC against the invoice-level reality. The fraud dataset flags **5 of 8
quarters**; the clean one flags none; the borderline one flags one. A flagged quarter is
where a supplier's claimed credit and their filed return disagree, and that disagreement is
the thing a credit committee cannot see by reading a spreadsheet.

## Chain of Evidence

Every ratio carries its own provenance — the document it came from, the page, the extraction
method and a confidence score — and the UI renders that as a citation badge rather than a
footnote. Twelve ratio cards per borrower, each with a four-point sparkline and a year-on-year
delta, all of them traceable.

This is the difference between an automated score and an auditable one. A score with no
provenance is a number a credit officer has to re-derive by hand; a score with provenance is
a number they can defend.

## Counterfactual reasoning

A decline is only useful if it comes with a way out. The counterfactual engine takes the
appraisal and prices the changes that would flip it, each with its impact, difficulty and
timeframe:

| Action | Impact | Difficulty | Timeframe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Repay ₹3 Cr of term debt — D/E 1.85× → 1.5× | +8 points | medium | 6 months |
| Cost optimisation — DSCR 1.25× → 1.5× | +11 points | hard | 9 months |
| Diversify buyers — top 3 below 35% | +6 points | medium | 12 months |
| Clear delayed MCA filings | +4 points | easy | 2 months |

The borrower gets a roadmap, not a verdict.

## The audit trail is part of the product

Not a log file — a typed record. Every event carries an actor, an actor type
(`ai_agent`, `human`, `system`), an action type, the module it touched, a confidence, and the
previous and new value. Human overrides are first-class objects with their own workflow
stage and compliance badge. When a file is challenged, the trail answers who changed what,
when, and on whose authority.

## Promoter intelligence

A D3 force-directed graph over promoter and counterparty relationships, with shell-company
and NPA-linked nodes flagged. Promoter reputation is one of the strongest predictors in a
credit file and one of the hardest to get at in a structured form.

## The memo

The Credit Appraisal Memo is assembled in six sections — executive summary, business
overview, financial analysis, promoter assessment, collateral assessment and risk
mitigation — with the fraud dataset swapping in a *fraud indicators* section and the
conditional dataset adding *conditions for approval*. Export is client-side jsPDF, with one
module per section.

## What is in the repository

The public repository is the front end: a React 18 + TypeScript + Vite SPA, roughly 11,900
lines across 10 routes and 12 page components, with about 1,800 lines of worked data for
three borrowers — an approve, a fraud and a conditional case — and a `DatasetContext` that
switches between them. `BACKEND_INTEGRATION_SPEC.md` documents the service architecture this
surface is built against; the specification is explicit that the shipped data path is demo
mode, and the front end is written against the typed contracts that mode will replace.
